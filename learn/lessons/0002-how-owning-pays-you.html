Lesson 2 · Owning a company

How owning pays you

The company's profit belongs to its owners, but it isn't posted to you. It reaches you in one of four ways.

Profit lands in the shop's bank account first. You part-own the shop, so you part-own that money. Here is how it can reach you.

RouteWhat happensMei's shop
DividendThe company pays out cash per share.Pays out $10,000 → $100 per piece.
BuybackThe company buys its own shares and cancels them. It pays you only if you choose to sell. If you keep yours, your slice gets bigger.Cancels 20 of 100 pieces → your piece is 1/80 instead of 1/100.
TakeoverAnother company buys every share at an agreed price. Your broker swaps your shares for cash automatically.KOI pays $4,000 per piece.
SellingYou sell your share to someone else at whatever price you agree.Someone pays you $3,000 for your piece.

No dividend doesn't mean no value

A company that keeps its profit to grow hasn't cancelled your share of it. It has delayed it. The new branches make bigger profits later, and those reach you through any of the routes above. Apple paid no dividends from 1995 to 2012, then started. Amazon and Berkshire Hathaway have never paid one.

A share that could never pay its owners anything, by any route, really would be worth nothing. Its price would only be people betting on other people. Your instinct there was right.

How buybacks and takeovers actually work

Buyback, usual kind: the company announces a budget, then quietly buys in the normal market at the normal price, alongside everyone else. You see nothing in your account. Buyback, rarer kind (tender offer): the company offers a set price for a set period. Your broker sends a notice, and you choose whether to sell.

Takeover: the buyer offers a price. To get owners to agree, it's usually above the market price, but below what the business will earn the buyer. Owners vote. If enough say yes, every owner is paid, including those who voted no.

Votes

Most shares carry a vote at the yearly owners' meeting (the AGM), on things like who runs the company. Votes don't depend on dividends. Voting papers arrive through your broker, often as an easy-to-miss notice.

Check yourself

1. Mei's shop pays out all of $10,000 profit. There are 100 pieces and you own 3. What do you get?

$10,000 ÷ 100 pieces = $100 per piece, × 3 pieces = $300.

2. The shop buys back and cancels 20 of its 100 pieces. You keep yours. Your slice of the business…

There are only 80 pieces left, so your 1 piece is 1/80 of everything instead of 1/100.

3. KOI's offer of $4,000 a piece is approved. You voted no. What happens to your pieces?

Once enough owners approve, everyone is paid the offer price and KOI owns the whole company.
